Abstract
Sudden unexpected infant death (SUID) has a characteristic age distribution, but most epidemiologic studies index age chronologically. We examined whether postconceptional age (PCA), the sum of gestational and postnatal age, clarifies how bed sharing, maternal smoking, and prematurity relate to SUID incidence. We analyzed 13,013 SUID cases (2008–2019) from the National Center for Fatality Review and Prevention Case Reporting System, modeling incidence across PCA with population-adjusted negative binomial regression. PCA alignment produced a clear rise-peak-decline pattern. Bed sharing was more strongly associated with incidence in an early developmental window (PCA <43 weeks; incidence rate ratio [IRR] 6.45, 95% CI 3.14–13.23) than a later one (PCA ≥47 weeks; IRR 2.10, 95% CI 1.70–2.58); maternal smoking and prematurity showed the same pattern while remaining positively associated throughout. Bed sharing’s association with incidence was further amplified by maternal smoking in the late window (interaction IRR 1.57, 95% CI 1.19–2.08). Because our prior developmental analysis identified sex-linked post-peak vulnerability, we repeated all models stratified by sex; associations were similar in males and females, indicating these effects are sex-independent. A developmental framework indicates that bed sharing confers disproportionate risk earlier in the postconceptional window, compounding with prenatal smoke exposure later in the window.
Developmental Timing of SUID and the Infant Sleep Environment
Introduction
Sudden unexpected infant death (SUID) remains a leading cause of post-neonatal mortality in the United States. Its age distribution is distinctive, with incidence rising in early infancy, peaking at approximately 2 to 4 months of chronological age, and then declining. This pattern has long been interpreted as consistent with a period of developmental vulnerability in homeostatic, cardiorespiratory, and arousal control systems [1-4]. However, this age distribution also reflects risk factors such as prenatal smoking, pregnancy or delivery complications, birthweight extremes, and short gestation, which shift the age distribution earlier once corrected for gestational age [5].
The infant sleep environment is another well-established correlate of SUID risk. Epidemiologic and policy literature has identified prone sleeping, soft bedding, sofa sleeping, and sleep-surface sharing as important exposures associated with sleep-related infant death risk [6, 7]. Among these, bed sharing has received particular attention because of its high prevalence and its interaction with other risk factors, including maternal smoking, prematurity, alcohol or drug exposure, and hazardous sleep settings. Case-control and pooled analyses suggest that the association between bed sharing and SUID varies by infant age and by co-occurring hazards, consistent with an age-dependent rather than uniform effect [8-10].
Many epidemiologic studies summarize infant age using chronological categories such as months of life or neonatal versus post-neonatal periods. These groupings may obscure developmental processes because infants are born at different gestational ages and therefore enter early infancy at different levels of physiologic maturity. Aligning infants by postconceptional age (PCA), defined as gestational age at birth plus postnatal age, provides a maturational time scale that may better clarify risk timing across infancy. Recent work examining the relation between gestational age and age at death in SUID also supports continued attention to developmental timing beyond postnatal age alone [11].
We used a PCA-based developmental framework to test whether bed sharing modifies the developmental timing of SUID incidence, adjusting for maternal smoking and preterm birth.

Methods
Data Sources
We conducted a retrospective analysis using the NCFRP Case Reporting System, a web-based surveillance platform developed to compile and analyze multidisciplinary fatality reviews of fetal, infant, and child deaths from participating jurisdictions in the United States. The system captures standardized information on death circumstances, infant characteristics, pregnancy factors, and sleep environment.
To estimate population denominators for exposure-adjusted models, we used 2 external data sources. Population distributions for maternal smoking during pregnancy and preterm birth were derived from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) National Vital Statistics System linked birth records [NVSS reference: National Center for Health Statistics. National Vital Statistics System, Natality public-use data 2008-2019, on CDC WONDER Online Database. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.]. Population prevalence of infant bed sharing was derived from PRAMS, a state- and jurisdiction-based surveillance system that uses stratified random sampling of women with recent live births and collects maternal self-reported information on infant care practices through standardized questionnaires [PRAMS reference: Shulman HB, D’Angelo DV, Harrison L, Smith RA, Warner L. The Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS): Overview of Design and Methodology. Am J Public Health. 2018;108(10):1305-1313.].

Case Definition
Cases were restricted to deaths classified as sudden infant death syndrome in the NCFRP database. Analyses were limited to infants who died within the first year of life and for whom gestational age at birth and postnatal age at death could be derived from available NCFRP fields. Blank, zero-coded, unknown, and implausible values in developmental and exposure variables were recoded to missing or unknown before analytic variable construction. Of 24,314 SUID cases in the NCFRP export, sequential exclusions removed 3,065 for death outside 2008–2019, 496 for death within the first 14 days of life, 140 for postconceptional age below the prespecified window, 6,113 for missing or invalid maternal smoking data, 20 for missing infant sex, and 1,467 for missing bed-sharing data, yielding the final analytic cohort of 13,013 cases (Figure 1). Deaths within the first 14 days of life were excluded to remove the acute perinatal-death period, consistent with our prior postconceptional-age analysis [Manuscript 1 citation]. Cases with unknown smoking status were excluded rather than imputed, because classifier-based imputation of a case-fatal exposure variable was judged more likely to introduce bias than to reduce it; this exclusion (6,113 of 20,613 candidate cases, 29.7%) was the single largest step in cohort construction.
Case Preprocessing and Developmental Age Alignment
NCFRP case records were preprocessed to standardize developmental variables before analysis. Postnatal age at death was derived from NCFRP age fields, converted to days, and then expressed in completed weeks. Gestational age at birth was obtained from NCFRP birth fields. PCA was defined as gestational age at birth plus postnatal age at death in completed weeks, placing infants on a unified developmental timeline referenced to biologic maturation rather than chronological age alone. Weekly case counts were computed across a prespecified analytic window of PCA 36 to 90 weeks, carried from our prior postconceptional-age analysis [Manuscript 1 citation] and verified against a count-stability criterion; boundary diagnostics and an upper-bound sensitivity analysis (PCA 79 weeks) are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.
Exposure Variables
Three exposure variables were included in the analysis: maternal smoking during pregnancy, preterm birth, and bed sharing.
Maternal smoking during pregnancy was derived from multiple NCFRP pregnancy smoking fields, including smoking before pregnancy, smoking at any time during pregnancy, and trimester-specific cigarette-use variables. Infants were classified as smoke-exposed if maternal smoking was recorded before pregnancy or during any trimester, unexposed if available smoking fields consistently indicated no exposure, and unknown otherwise. This construction reflects the preprocessing logic used in the project pipeline rather than reliance on a single raw smoking field.
Preterm birth was defined as gestational age at birth less than 37 completed weeks.
Case-level bed sharing was classified from NCFRP death-scene sleep-environment variables rather than from PRAMS. Direct evidence of same-surface sharing was retained as bed sharing. To reduce exposure misclassification, records with initially unknown bed-sharing status were further evaluated using auxiliary sleep-environment fields. Records coded as unknown were reclassified as non–bed sharing when auxiliary sleep-environment fields indicated solitary sleep. Thus, NCFRP supplied the case exposure classification, whereas PRAMS supplied the population prevalence used for denominator construction. Bed sharing and infant sex were treated as non-imputable ground-truth variables; cases missing either (20 for sex, 1,467 for bed-sharing status) were excluded rather than assigned an inferred value.
For the denominator, population prevalence of bed sharing was estimated using PRAMS sleep-practice data. In PRAMS, bed sharing was operationalized from maternal responses regarding whether the infant slept alone in their own sleep surface, with responses indicating “always” or “almost always” sleeping alone treated as non–bed sharing and other responses treated as some degree of sleep-surface sharing. This denominator should therefore be interpreted as a population proxy for habitual or meaningful bed-sharing exposure, not as a direct measure of terminal-sleep bed sharing. PRAMS questionnaire phase and item wording vary somewhat by state and year, which may introduce some heterogeneity into the bed-sharing prevalence estimate used for the denominator.
Population Denominators and Regression Offsets
Because the NCFRP database is a case-based fatality review surveillance system rather than a population registry, population denominators for exposure-stratified analyses were estimated from external sources. Population counts for strata defined by maternal smoking during pregnancy and preterm birth were derived from national live-birth data. Population prevalence of bed sharing was estimated separately from PRAMS survey responses using the dichotomized definition above. Exposure-specific denominators were incorporated into regression models through log-transformed offsets, allowing incidence rate ratios (IRRs) to be estimated relative to the underlying population distribution of each exposure; because case counts and denominators come from different sources, these IRRs are best interpreted as directional and relative rather than as absolute population incidence rates (Limitations).
Statistical Analysis
Weekly SUID case counts were aggregated into PCA × bed sharing × smoking × prematurity strata and modeled using negative binomial regression to account for overdispersion. Primary models were parameterized using PCA in weeks as the developmental time variable and adjusted for maternal smoking during pregnancy, preterm birth, and bed sharing, with exposure-specific offsets included to account for denominator structure.
Because the descriptive developmental trajectory showed a rise, peak, and decline pattern, confirmatory negative binomial models were fit separately to an early rising phase (PCA below 43 weeks; n = 2,646) and a later declining phase (PCA 47 weeks and above; n = 7,391), together comprising the windowed analytic population. Both windowed models included prespecified bed sharing by maternal smoking and bed sharing by preterm birth product terms, which were retained regardless of statistical significance because they were specified in advance. The peak interval (PCA 43 to 46 weeks; n = 2,976) was summarized descriptively and excluded from the piecewise regressions because of week-to-week instability at the apex. A full-curve negative binomial model incorporating a bed sharing by PCA varying-coefficient interaction spline was also fit to characterize the developmental trajectory continuously across PCA. Adjusted IRRs with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for model terms. Sex-stratified, birthweight-stratified, and smoking-stratified analyses were conducted as sensitivity analyses.
Because most NCFRP cases (91%) report infant age in completed months rather than days, converting months to weeks under a 30-day-month convention introduces a spurious periodicity of approximately 4.2 weeks in the raw weekly counts, reflecting calendar arithmetic rather than a biological cycle. This does not affect inference: the windowed models aggregate into 4-week-or-larger windows that absorb it, the negative binomial dispersion parameter accounts for residual overdispersion, and the GAM smooth averages over it. The moving-average overlay in Figure 2 is a display aid only; all models are fit on raw integer counts.
Sensitivity Analyses
Sensitivity analyses evaluated the robustness of the bed-sharing denominator classification by exploring alternative thresholds for solitary-sleep responses in the PRAMS-derived sleep-practice variable. Results were compared with the primary analysis to assess whether estimated associations and developmental trajectories were sensitive to the operational definition of bed sharing.

Results
Case selection and cohort characteristics
From the full NCFRP case export, 13,013 SUID cases with complete developmental and exposure data formed the analytic cohort (Figure 1), after exclusions detailed in Methods. Cases were aligned by PCA and aggregated into weekly strata defined by bed sharing, maternal smoking, and prematurity, restricted to strata with at least 10 cases. As in our prior PCA analysis [Manuscript 1 citation], the age trajectory showed a rise, peak, and decline structure spanning the prespecified early (n = 2,646), peak (n = 2,976, descriptive only), and late (n = 7,391) windows (Methods).
Characteristics of the full analytic cohort by bed-sharing status are shown in Table 1. Bed sharing was recorded for 7,739 cases (59.5%) and was absent for 5,274 (40.5%). Bed-sharing cases more often involved maternal smoking during pregnancy (52.2% versus 40.8%) and were concentrated earlier in development, with a larger share in the early PCA window (25.7% versus 12.5%) and dying before one month of age (37.5% versus 18.0%). A cause-of-death code of accidental suffocation or strangulation was assigned less often among bed-sharing cases (6.1% versus 15.0%). Overall, 47.6% of cases involved maternal smoking during pregnancy and 21.2% were born preterm, including 2.7% born at less than 32 weeks' gestation.
Association between bed sharing and smoking among cases
Bed sharing and maternal smoking during pregnancy co-occurred frequently within the case series. In a contingency analysis restricted to cases, bed sharing was more common among infants whose mothers smoked during pregnancy than among those whose mothers did not (χ² = 162.1, df = 1, P < .001); 3,701 of 6,822 cases with non-smoking mothers involved bed sharing, compared with 4,038 of 6,191 cases with mothers who smoked. Because this analysis conditions on being a fatal case with no surviving comparison group, it describes co-occurrence rather than risk; the risk question is addressed by the offset regression models and the prespecified bed sharing by smoking interaction. This pattern is consistent with other studies linking smoking to SUID risk [13], and, more recently, vaping [15].
Chronological age patterns
On the postnatal age scale, weekly incidence was highest in the first several months of life and then declined (Figure 2A). Infants reported to be bed sharing consistently showed higher descriptive incidence than those not reported to be bed sharing, although the postnatal-age representation remained more irregular and less developmentally coherent than the PCA-aligned curves. This reinforced the limitation of chronological age as the primary developmental axis and motivated use of PCA for subsequent modeling.
Developmental age pattern
When aligned by PCA, weekly incidence followed a clearer developmental structure, with a steep rise across the early weeks, a peak in the mid-40-week PCA range, and a gradual decline thereafter (Figure 2B). Bed-sharing and non–bed-sharing curves shared the same broad developmental shape, but the bed-sharing curve was consistently shifted upward, indicating higher incidence across much of the developmental trajectory rather than a localized effect around the peak. In addition, the bed-sharing trajectory appeared to reach its maximum earlier and more sharply, whereas the non–bed-sharing trajectory showed a broader peak later in development (Figure 3). This PCA-aligned representation sharpened the developmental pattern and provided the basis for the piecewise and full-curve models.
Full-curve GAM results
A negative binomial model incorporating a bed sharing by PCA varying-coefficient interaction spline was fit to characterize the developmental trajectory continuously across PCA (Figure 3). Bed sharing (IRR 3.47, 95% CI 3.05–3.94; P < .001), maternal smoking (IRR 11.31, 95% CI 9.94–12.87; P < .001), and preterm birth (IRR 1.85, 95% CI 1.59–2.17; P < .001) were each independently associated with higher fitted incidence across the curve, consistent with the windowed early/late contrast reported below.
Confirmatory windowed models
Windowed negative binomial models showed the expected developmental reversal in PCA-associated risk: incidence increased with PCA in the early window (IRR 1.19 per week, P = .004) and declined with PCA in the late window (IRR 0.923 per week, P < .001). Bed sharing (early IRR 6.45, late 2.10), maternal smoking (early 12.48, late 8.33), and preterm birth (early 3.20, late 1.66) were each independently associated with higher incidence in both windows, all P < .001 (Figure 4); the preterm association roughly halved from early to late while remaining significant throughout.
Prespecified interaction terms indicated that the association between bed sharing and SUID incidence was modified by maternal smoking in the late developmental window, where the bed sharing by smoking interaction was significant (IRR 1.57, 95% CI 1.19–2.08; P = .001). The corresponding early-window bed sharing by smoking term was not significant (IRR 1.13, 95% CI 0.56–2.28; P = .73), and neither the early nor the late bed sharing by preterm birth interaction was significant (early IRR 1.03, 95% CI 0.49–2.15; late IRR 1.05, 95% CI 0.75–1.46). All four prespecified interaction estimates, with confidence intervals and P values, are reported in Supplementary Table 3.
Sex-stratified sensitivity analyses
Sex-stratified sensitivity analyses showed that the developmental and exposure-associated structure of risk was highly similar in females and males. Full sex-stratified estimates for PCA, bed sharing, maternal smoking, and preterm birth in both windows are provided in Supplementary Table 4.
Birthweight-stratified sensitivity analyses
As a further sensitivity analysis, models were stratified by low birthweight (<2,500 g); bed sharing was independently associated with higher incidence in both strata where estimable, and the prespecified bed sharing by low-birthweight interaction was not significant in either window, providing no evidence that bed sharing is differentially hazardous for low-birthweight infants (Supplementary Table 5).
Smoking-stratified sensitivity analyses
Models stratified by maternal smoking during pregnancy showed no early-window effect modification (bed sharing IRR 6.94 among non-smokers versus 6.92 among smokers), but a larger late-window bed-sharing association among smokers (IRR 3.35) than non-smokers (IRR 2.07), concentrating the interaction identified above in the late developmental window; bed sharing was also associated with a higher share of deaths before one month of age in both smoking strata (Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion
In this developmental analysis of SUID using PCA as the primary time scale, bed sharing and maternal smoking during pregnancy were strongly associated with higher incidence across both the rising and declining phases of the developmental curve. Prematurity was positively associated with incidence in both windows, more strongly in the early window. When incidence was aligned by PCA rather than chronological age, the developmental pattern became clearer, with a steep early rise, a peak in the mid-40-week PCA range (the classical 2-to-4-month chronological-age peak re-expressed on the maturational axis), and a gradual decline thereafter. This suggests that sleep-environment risk is more coherently interpreted against physiologic maturity than postnatal time alone.
A central finding is that bed sharing did not behave like a narrow, peak-limited hazard. Instead, the SUID risk attributable to bed sharing was elevated across much of the developmental trajectory, and this pattern persisted after adjustment for smoking and prematurity. In both the piecewise and full-curve models, bed sharing remained strongly associated with higher incidence, although its magnitude was smaller than that of maternal smoking. These results do not support the idea that bed sharing is relevant only at a single developmental transition or in the presence of one specific background vulnerability [9, 12]; rather, it appears to act as a broadly distributed environmental stressor superimposed on a time-structured period of developmental susceptibility, concentrated in the earlier portion of the vulnerable window (Figure 3).
Maternal smoking during pregnancy showed the largest and most consistent association with SUID incidence in both windows and both sexes. This is consistent with long-standing epidemiologic evidence identifying prenatal smoke exposure as one of the strongest modifiable correlates of sudden infant death [7, 13]. By contrast, prematurity remained a positive and significant correlate across the developmental window, though roughly twice as strong early as late, consistent with immaturity at birth exerting its greatest influence in the earliest developmental stages without disappearing later. In the late developmental window, bed sharing and prenatal smoke exposure combined super-multiplicatively: the bed-sharing association was larger among smokers (IRR 3.35) than non-smokers (IRR 2.07). In the early window, where bed sharing already carried a large independent effect, smoking status did not further modify it (IRR 6.94 versus 6.92).
These findings also help clarify how this sleep-environment framework relates to our prior developmental PCA analysis of sex differences in SUID. In the earlier study, male excess emerged after the developmental peak and persisted across the declining phase, suggesting a sex-linked component of post-peak vulnerability. Here, once bed sharing, smoking, and prematurity were explicitly modeled, the developmental structure of risk was similar in girls and boys.
This distinction matters conceptually: it does not negate the possibility of sex-linked differences in arousal, respiratory control, autonomic regulation, or survival after other metabolic or environmental challenge, but indicates that the timing and magnitude of risk associated with bed sharing and prenatal smoke exposure are not primarily organized by sex.
The use of PCA is central to that interpretation. Chronological age groups infants by time since birth, but not developmental maturity, a distinction that is biologically consequential given SUID's widely posited period of developmental vulnerability [4]. In this study, PCA alignment sharpened the rise-peak-decline trajectory and clarified how bed sharing and smoking relate to that curve. PCA and chronological age were highly correlated in this cohort (Pearson r = 0.969, Spearman r = 0.940), as expected given that PCA incorporates postnatal age directly.
This distinction bears directly on an alternative reading of these findings: that bed sharing is simply an environmental hazard that happens to be more common or more consequential in young infants, with no need to invoke a developmental mechanism [8]. Because bed-sharing deaths concentrate early on both time scales, chronological age alone cannot distinguish this account from a developmental one: the proportion of cases born preterm falls sharply across PCA, from effectively all cases at the lowest PCA weeks to roughly 10 to 15% by the mid-40-week range, while the same proportion is roughly flat, near 20%, across chronological age. Chronological age is therefore a poor proxy for the maturational composition it is meant to stand in for, particularly in the earliest postnatal weeks, where infants of very different gestational ages are grouped together. Aligning by PCA resolves this heterogeneity and yields a smooth, roughly symmetric rise-peak-decline incidence curve, consistent with a single underlying developmental process rather than an artifact of chronological time since birth. A direct model-fit comparison, reported in Supplementary Table 7, corroborates this: the PCA-aligned fit has substantially lower deviance than the chronological-age fit.
These two accounts are not mutually exclusive. Bed sharing is not intrinsically hazardous; sleep-surface sharing practiced without accompanying hazards is common and is not, by itself, the subject of this analysis. Prevailing infant-care guidance in the United States nonetheless favors a separate sleep surface, particularly for young, preterm, or low-birthweight infants [6]. Bed sharing that persists against this guidance for the most physiologically vulnerable infants may be better understood as a marker of unmet or unavailable safe-sleep resources than as a uniform hazard. Under this reading, bed sharing functions as an environmental exposure whose prevalence and consequence are themselves developmentally patterned, concentrated among the youngest and least mature infants. The developmental and environmental accounts are therefore complementary rather than competing: bed sharing's association with SUID is strongest where it co-occurs with developmental vulnerability, whether that vulnerability arises from prematurity, young postconceptional age, or both.
Limitations
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was an observational analysis of registry-based case data, and the resulting estimates are associative rather than causal. Second, sleep-environment classification in case-report systems may be subject to heterogeneity in ascertainment and reporting. Third, the bed-sharing denominator was derived indirectly from external prevalence estimates rather than a source linked to the cases, introducing potential ascertainment mismatch (see also the coverage limitation below). Fourth, the analytic strategy intentionally excluded sparse strata and zero-count cells to stabilize estimation, meaning the fitted models describe observed high-information strata rather than a fully enumerated population lattice. Finally, the peak interval was treated descriptively rather than modeled directly in the windowed regressions because of week-to-week instability, which limits inference specifically at the apex of the curve. In addition, because SUID case counts derive from a fatality-review series and exposure denominators from national data, the exposure IRRs, and the maternal smoking IRR in particular, are not directly comparable in magnitude to case-control effect estimates. The largest single exclusion in cohort construction was missing or invalid maternal smoking status (6,113 cases, 29.7% of candidates remaining after the developmental-window restrictions); these cases were excluded rather than imputed, which assumes that smoking-status missingness is unrelated to the outcome within the observed cohort.
This analysis has several additional considerations regarding coverage and representativeness. The cohort spans 12 death years (2008–2019); restricting to this window removed 3,065 cases from the raw NCFRP export. Jurisdictional participation in NCFRP grew over the study period, with the share of cases from later years increasing (19.4% in 2008–2011, 35.8% in 2012–2015, 44.8% in 2016–2019), so the case series should be understood as a large, growing multi-jurisdiction surveillance sample rather than a fixed national census. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates approximately 3,400 SUID deaths annually in the United States [CDC reference]; over a comparable 12-year period this would total on the order of 40,000 deaths, so the 13,013 cases analyzed here represent roughly one-third of national SUID over the study window. Because population denominators for smoking, prematurity, and bed sharing were drawn from national surveillance sources rather than matched to NCFRP's participating jurisdictions, the absolute incidence scale shown in Figure 2 is a population proxy rather than a jurisdiction-matched rate, and time-varying exposure prevalence over the study period was not modeled.
Cause-of-death classification in NCFRP is broad: cases are coded as undetermined (40.8%), accidental suffocation or strangulation (9.7%), natural or medical (46.4%), or other (3.1%). The split between suffocation and undetermined codes is diagnostic-practice-dependent and can vary by jurisdiction and year. The lower share of accidental-suffocation coding among bed-sharing cases relative to non-bed-sharing cases (6.1% versus 15.0%, Table 1) reflects this ascertainment pattern rather than evidence that bed sharing lowers suffocation risk. Suffocation subtype, such as overlay, wedging, or soft bedding, is not reliably codeable at this sample size, so no fine-grained mechanistic claim about the pathway from bed sharing to death is made here.
Despite these limitations, these findings support a coherent developmental-exposure model of SUID in which modifiable risk factors act on a common period of vulnerability shared across sexes, rather than through strongly sex-specific or purely chronological-age-based mechanisms.

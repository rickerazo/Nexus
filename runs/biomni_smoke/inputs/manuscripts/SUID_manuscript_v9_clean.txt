Abstract
Objectives: To test whether male excess risk in sudden unexpected infant death (SUID) reflects a constant effect from birth or a developmentally timed phenomenon by applying a postconceptional age (PCA) alignment framework that accounts for differences in maturity at birth.
Methods: Population-based analysis of US CDC linked birth–infant death records from 2014 to 2021. SUID was defined using ICD-10 codes R95, R99, and W75, excluding deaths in the first postnatal week. Infant age was aligned using PCA (gestational age at birth plus postnatal age). Weekly SUID counts across PCA weeks 36 to 90 were modelled using a negative binomial generalised additive model with spline smooths and an offset based on national live-birth proportions. Prespecified negative binomial models compared an early rising window (36–42 PCA weeks) and a late declining window (≥47 PCA weeks).
Results: Among 23,686 eligible SUID cases, PCA-aligned incidence revealed a clear rise, peak, and decline pattern. Male and female trajectories overlapped during the rising phase and peak. Sex divergence emerged only after the peak, with persistently higher male incidence during the declining phase. Confirmatory models showed similar male and female incidence in the early window and higher male incidence in the late window (Incidence rate ratios 1.14 early vs 1.36 late). Maternal smoking was associated with higher incidence across both windows.
Conclusions: Aligning infants on a developmental time scale demonstrates that male excess in SUID is not present from birth but emerges after the incidence peak. Aligning infants by PCA refines the interpretation of SUID risk patterns and can improve the targeting of prevention and mechanistic research.
Developmental Timing of Male Excess in Sudden Unexpected Infant Death
Introduction
Sudden unexpected infant death (SUID) peaks in early infancy and remains a major public health concern [1, 2]. This well-recognised temporal pattern, with highest incidence between approximately 1 and 3 months of age, has long been interpreted as reflecting a period of developmental vulnerability [3-6]. Male sex is also a consistent risk factor, but whether the male excess is constant or emerges during a specific developmental period remains unclear.
A central limitation in addressing this question is how infant age is represented. Most epidemiologic studies summarise age using chronological postnatal categories, such as months of life or neonatal versus postneonatal periods [7, 8]. However, chronological age is an imperfect proxy for biological maturation because infants are born at different gestational ages. As a result, analyses based on postnatal age alone may conflate developmental processes with variation in maturity at birth, potentially obscuring when risk transitions occur.
Prior work has examined SUID timing in relation to corrected age and gestational maturity. Allen et al. [9] showed that gestational age and age at death are related in SUID, and Lavista Ferres et al. [10] demonstrated that SUID separates into statistically distinct early neonatal (days 0–6) and postperinatal (days 7–364) subpopulations with differing risk-factor profiles, implying a discrete shift in underlying risk rather than a continuous process. This body of work supports the relevance of developmental alignment, but it did not directly test whether male excess risk is constant across infancy or emerges after a specific developmental transition.
From an epidemiologic perspective, this is fundamentally an alignment problem: infants enter postnatal life at different points along a shared developmental trajectory. A time scale that aligns individuals by biological age rather than time since birth may therefore provide a more appropriate representation of risk. Postconceptional age (PCA), defined as gestational age at birth plus postnatal age, offers such a framework by placing infants on a unified developmental axis while preserving preterm birth as a distinct covariate.
Applying PCA allows risk patterns to be interpreted in relation to developmental timing rather than chronological age. This distinction is particularly important for questions involving temporal transitions, such as whether male excess risk is present from the earliest weeks or emerges after a specific developmental threshold.
In this study, we use PCA to align infants on a developmental time scale and estimate sex-specific SUID risk trajectories across early infancy. Infant sex is the primary exposure of interest, and PCA is the developmental time scale over which sex-specific incidence trajectories are estimated. We test the hypothesis that male excess risk is developmentally timed rather than constant across infancy. To do so, we first fit a full-curve negative binomial generalised additive model across PCA weeks to estimate smooth trajectories and then apply prespecified windowed negative binomial models to obtain clinically interpretable incidence rate ratios during early rising and late declining developmental periods. Maternal smoking and preterm birth were included as covariates because both are established risk factors for SUID, and preterm birth shifts the postnatal age at death [7, 9].
Methods
Study design and data source
The study used de-identified publicly available data and was deemed exempt from institutional review board oversight.
We conducted a retrospective, population-based incidence study using the US CDC linked birth–infant death records from 2014 to 2021, encompassing live births and infant deaths across the United States and US territories [8]. 
Case definition
SUID cases were identified by underlying cause-of-death ICD-10 codes R95 (Sudden Infant Death Syndrome [SIDS]), W75 (Accidental suffocation and strangulation in bed), and R99 (Other ill-defined and unspecified causes of mortality). Deaths occurring during the first seven postnatal days were excluded to focus on postperinatal vulnerability beyond the first postnatal week.
Age scales
Postnatal age was summarised by completed postnatal months for conventional monthly rate ratios and by weeks for descriptive rate curves. PCA was defined in weeks as gestational age at birth plus postnatal age at death and served as the primary developmental time scale.
Exposure and covariates
Infant sex was the primary exposure of interest, and PCA was the developmental time scale over which sex-specific SUID incidence trajectories were estimated. PCA was calculated as gestational age at birth plus postnatal age and was therefore not treated as an exposure or covariate. Preterm birth and maternal smoking during pregnancy (any reported smoking) were included as covariates because both are established risk factors for SUID, and preterm birth shifts the postnatal age at death [9]. Preterm birth was defined as a binary variable (<37 versus ≥37 completed gestational weeks); continuous gestational age was not additionally included as a covariate because gestational age at birth contributes directly to the calculation of PCA. Prespecified interaction terms between smoking and preterm birth and between sex and preterm birth were included to assess whether these associations differed according to preterm status. Thus, the analysis examined whether the association between sex and SUID incidence varied across developmental age while accounting for preterm birth and maternal smoking.
Because deaths in the first postnatal week were excluded, infants born at term could not enter the analytic cohort before PCA week 39; PCA weeks 36–38 therefore include only preterm infants (n=469) by construction.
Breastfeeding was not included as a covariate. Although breastfeeding is associated with lower SUID risk, the objective of this analysis was to examine whether the male–female difference in SUID incidence varies across developmental age rather than to estimate the independent effects of all established SUID risk factors. In addition, breastfeeding is a postnatal, time-varying exposure. To assess whether breastfeeding status modified the sex-specific trajectories, we repeated the full analysis separately among infants breastfed and not breastfed at discharge, using population offsets derived from live births with the same breastfeeding status, and compared windowed-model estimates between groups using a Wald test of the difference in log incidence rate ratios.
Denominators and offsets
Weekly case counts were computed within strata defined by PCA week, sex, maternal smoking, and preterm birth. The offset for each stratum was the logarithm of the product of the national proportions of live births in that stratum's sex, maternal smoking, and preterm-birth categories (US live births, 2014–2021), which treats these three characteristics as independent in the birth population. Models used a log link.
Analytic cohort and PCA range
The analytic PCA range was defined using a count-stability criterion requiring at least five male and at least five female SUID deaths per PCA week. Applying this criterion yielded an analytic range of PCA weeks 36–90. Below PCA week 36, 9 of the 12 observed weeks failed to meet this criterion; above PCA week 90, all 8 observed weeks failed to meet it. In contrast, all weeks within the analytic range met the criterion. Counts outside this range included recurrent sparse or zero sex-specific cells, limiting model stability and interpretability. Such counts are below the number required for stable estimation in the negative binomial model used in the present study; in this model, all stratified bins must include cases, and if some bins contain zero cases for females or males, the model becomes unstable and unreliable. Under this analytic window, exclusions were small at both tails (110 infants, 0.44% of the 25,257, for PCA <36; 28 infants, 0.11%, for PCA >90). The 43 to 46 week interval corresponded to the empiric peak, where incidence turns from rising to declining; peak timing varies with perinatal factors [9].
The descriptive rate curves and the full-curve generalised additive model used the complete analytic cohort across PCA weeks 36–90, including the 43–46-week peak interval. The peak interval was excluded only from the confirmatory windowed negative binomial regressions, in which it was summarised descriptively instead, because a log-linear PCA term cannot represent the change from rising to declining incidence.
Primary model: Full-curve generalised additive model
To estimate a single smooth developmental risk trajectory across PCA, we fit a negative binomial generalised additive model with spline smooths over PCA. Sex-specific trajectories were estimated and contrasted across PCA weeks. Model-based sex contrasts were summarised as fitted differences and fitted male-to-female ratios across PCA.
Confirmatory model: Prespecified windowed negative binomial regression
Because the PCA trajectory includes distinct phases, we performed confirmatory windowed negative binomial regressions to provide clinically interpretable incidence rate ratios within two prespecified developmental windows: an early rising phase (PCA weeks 36 to 42) and a late declining phase (PCA weeks 47 or more). The 43 to 46 week peak was summarised descriptively because incidence turns from rising to declining within it.
Conventional calibration
For comparability with conventional reporting, we summarised monthly postnatal age-based rates and rate ratios by sex, maternal smoking, and preterm birth.
Results
Case selection and cohort characteristics
From 25,257 SUID deaths, 23,686 met case-selection and PCA-window criteria (Figure 1); Their characteristics are shown in Table 1. Of these, 19,248 fell outside the 43–46-week peak interval and were included in the confirmatory windowed regressions: 2,946 deaths in the early window (<43 weeks) and 16,302 in the late window (≥47 weeks). The 4,438 deaths in the peak interval were summarised descriptively.
Chronological age patterns
On the postnatal age scale, incidence peaked between 1 and 3 months, and approximately two thirds of deaths occurred within the first four months of life (Figure 2A) [7, 8]. Monthly male-to-female rate ratios were near unity during the first completed month and increased thereafter, remaining elevated through much of infancy (Figure 2A) [4]. Preterm birth was associated with higher incidence across most of infancy [7].
Maternal smoking was independently associated with higher odds of preterm birth in our population denominator (odds ratio 1.48; n=30,656,644 live births, 2014–2021), yet the prespecified Smoking×Preterm interaction term was non-significant in both the early and late developmental windows (Supplemental Tables 1 and 4), indicating that smoking's association with SUID incidence did not differ appreciably by preterm/term status.
Developmental age pattern
When aligned by PCA, weekly incidence followed a rise, peak, and decline structure, with a sharp increase from 36 to 42 weeks, a brief peak around 43 to 46 weeks, and a gradual decline thereafter (Figure 2B). Male and female rates overlapped early but diverged after the peak, with higher male incidence throughout the declining phase (Figure 2B). The proportion of preterm cases declined sharply across early PCA weeks and stabilised later.
The 43–46-week peak is itself a composite rather than the product of a single mechanism. Supplemental Figure 1 illustrates the differential contribution of term and preterm infants to the peak: the case count by chronological age is approximately normally distributed for term infants but right-skewed for preterm infants. These shifted term and preterm distributions combine to produce the non-linearities around the peak that motivated modelling the early and late phases separately. We consider this heterogeneity a substantive finding in its own right, one generally obscured by the continuous models conventionally used to characterise SUID mortality curves.
Full-curve generalised additive model results
In the full-curve negative binomial generalised additive model, fitted male and female trajectories closely overlapped during the early rising phase and around the peak (Figure 3A). After the peak, a clear divergence emerged, with persistently higher fitted male incidence across the declining phase (Figure 3A). Model-based contrasts showed minimal sex differences early and sustained male excess later (Figure 3B-C).
Confirmatory windowed models
Windowed negative binomial models quantified this transition (Figure 4). In the early window, male incidence was similar to female incidence (incidence rate ratio [IRR] 1.14, 95% CI 0.85–1.53; Figure 4A; Supplemental Table 1). Sensitivity analyses shifting the lower bound from 36 to 38 PCA weeks yielded comparable estimates for sex (IRR 1.14, 95% CI 0.83–1.57), smoking, preterm birth, and the PCA slope, indicating that the early developmental interval is robust to alternative window definitions (Supplemental Table 1). Maternal smoking (IRR 7.22, 95% CI 5.36–9.73) and preterm birth (IRR 7.14, 95% CI 5.08–10.04) were associated with higher incidence in the early window (Figure 4A). Profile-specific relative risks and derived absolute risks for combinations of sex, preterm birth, and smoking are provided in Supplemental Table 2.
In the late window, male incidence exceeded female incidence (IRR 1.36, 95% CI 1.29–1.42; Figure 4B; Supplemental Table 4). Maternal smoking remained associated with higher incidence (IRR 4.55, 95% CI 4.32–4.79), while the preterm association was attenuated (IRR 1.47, 95% CI 1.35–1.59), consistent with selective survival into later infancy. Profile-specific relative risks and derived absolute risks for this developmental interval are shown in Supplemental Table 3. The prespecified Sex×Preterm interaction estimates were similar in the early (IRR 1.11, 95% CI 0.76–1.62) and late windows (IRR 1.12, 95% CI 1.02–1.24; P = 0.018), indicating a somewhat larger male excess among preterm infants later in infancy (Supplemental Tables 1 and 4).
In the sensitivity analysis stratified by breastfeeding status at discharge, the male-to-female IRR did not differ between breastfed and non-breastfed infants in either the early window (IRR 1.08, 95% CI 0.81–1.46 versus 1.24, 95% CI 0.89–1.73; P for difference = 0.55) or the late window (IRR 1.35, 95% CI 1.28–1.42 versus 1.36, 95% CI 1.27–1.45; P for difference = 0.82).
Discussion
This study reframes the well-established male predominance in SUID as a developmentally timed phenomenon and, importantly, demonstrates how this interpretation depends on the choice of epidemiologic time scale. When infants are aligned on a developmental age scale using PCA, SUID incidence follows a rise, peak, and decline pattern in which the male–female difference is not uniform across the trajectory. There is no male excess during the early rising phase, and male excess emerges only after the developmental peak. Chronological age also shows no male excess in the first postnatal weeks (Figure 2A) [4], but on that scale the incidence curve begins at about half its peak rate, so the rising phase cannot be modelled and the emergence of the sex difference cannot be tied to a developmental stage. Framing risk on a developmental time scale provides a clearer and more precise clinical message than treating male sex as a constant risk factor from birth.
From an epidemiologic perspective, aligning infants by PCA addresses a key limitation of chronological age: infants are born at different levels of physiologic maturity and therefore enter postnatal life at different points along the developmental trajectory. PCA aligns infants according to developmental age by combining gestational age at birth with postnatal age. Preterm birth can nevertheless be retained as a separate binary covariate (<37 versus ≥37 completed gestational weeks), allowing comparison of infants born preterm and at term at the same PCA. Continuous gestational age was not additionally included as a predictor because it contributes directly to the calculation of PCA. In this framework, the association with preterm birth is strongest early, when overall incidence is rising and developmental regulation is rapidly changing [7]. Its attenuation later in infancy is likely to reflect survivorship and selection, and it shows that the association with a characteristic present at birth can change across developmental time.
Maternal smoking remained one of the strongest risk factors for SUID throughout development [3]. Clinically, this supports continued prioritisation of smoking-related prevention and cessation before and during pregnancy, given that absolute risk is highest in early infancy. Because the smoking association is strong in both developmental windows, it is unlikely to explain the timing of the male divergence by itself; instead it likely acts as a general vulnerability amplifier across the developmental trajectory. Within the PCA framework, smoking appears to elevate baseline risk without altering the timing of the peak (Supplemental Figure 1B) or the emergence of sex differences, reinforcing the distinction between factors that shift overall incidence and those that modify developmental transitions.
Mechanistically, it remains unclear why male excess emerges later during development, but the alignment of epidemiologic timing with experimental observations provides useful constraints. Human, sex-stratified mechanistic evidence explicitly aligned to PCA remains limited, even though animal studies suggest that sex-linked vulnerability can emerge across postnatal development. In animal models, post-hypoxic recovery of the respiratory rhythm is sex dependent, with males demonstrating impaired recovery under hypoxic challenge, implicating sex-linked vulnerability in autoresuscitation circuits rather than baseline breathing differences [11]. Notably, the sex difference in post-hypoxic recovery is not present in the youngest postnatal age bins and reappears at older postnatal ages, consistent with a developmentally emergent phenotype rather than a fixed effect from birth [11]. One plausible explanation for the emergence of the male excess is prenatal stress exposure, which is known to produce sex-specific disruption of respiratory control maturation and increased apnea propensity. Rat studies demonstrate that early-life exposures can program postnatal respiratory instability in a sex-dependent manner [12]. Stress, in particular separation from the mother, interferes with laryngeal chemoreflex maturation, and the male bias in excessive cardiorespiratory inhibition develops later in postnatal development, with exaggerated laryngeal chemoreflex-mediated apnea, desaturation, and bradycardia at postnatal day 14 to 15 (P14 – P15) after neonatal maternal separation, particularly in males [13]. This timing overlaps the rat critical period of respiratory network vulnerability centered around P12 to P13, which has been proposed to correspond developmentally to the human SIDS peak at roughly 2 to 4 months [14]. Within this window, coordinated shifts toward reduced excitation and increased inhibition have been discussed across brainstem respiratory nuclei and hypoglossal motor control circuits [15, 16]. Thus, it is possible that early life stress, such as separation from the mother, could be one factor that predisposes males to increased vulnerability to SIDS. However, all these studies are based on rodent models and may not directly translate to humans. Thus, many questions remain unsolved and further research will be necessary to study the long-term consequences of separation from the mother in human infants. Separation that occurs in the neonatal intensive care unit, for example, may have different consequences than separation occurring later in infancy, such as at 2 months of age. Also, although our outcome definition spans the SUID umbrella, we use SIDS-focused neurophysiology and neuropathology as plausible substrates for the developmental timing pattern because these pathways directly implicate arousal, autoresuscitation, and brainstem autonomic control.
Another important clue comes from human neuropathologic data indicating that medullary serotonergic receptor binding in SIDS varies with developmental age. Key brainstem regulatory systems undergo structured maturational changes during infancy [17], providing a plausible substrate for the transition observed across PCA windows, as serotonergic modulation of cardiorespiratory homeostasis evolves during the peak-to-late developmental interval. Marked early-life steroid dynamics in preterm infants highlight a rapidly changing endocrine milieu across early development and further support the value of PCA alignment, particularly for understanding how preterm birth may shift the timing of vulnerability [18]. Broader stress–neuroendocrine models integrate prenatal exposures, endocrine maturation, and sex-biased respiratory development into a unified framework of vulnerability that could underlie the emergence of male excess in SIDS [19].
Taken together, these data support a two-stage interpretation of our epidemiologic findings that depends on developmental time alignment. The early PCA window appears dominated by exposure-sensitive systems, consistent with prenatal programming of respiratory instability [12] and strong effects of smoking and preterm birth. The later window reveals sustained male excess, consistent with developmentally emergent sex differences in hypoxia recovery and stress-related reflex vulnerability [11, 13], occurring against a background of age-structured serotonergic maturation [17, 20] and a conserved critical period of respiratory network reorganisation [14-16]. By aligning individuals on a common developmental axis, PCA clarifies that these phases represent distinct segments of a single underlying trajectory rather than independent age categories.
Importantly, our data localise the timing of the sex divergence and generate testable hypotheses about the physiologic pathways involved. The interpretation that this divergence is mediated by serotonergic, endocrine, and stress-related factors affecting hypoxia-recovery mechanisms is further supported by human pathological studies linking SIDS to socioeconomic factors such as lack of access to communication (e.g. absence of a phone as a proxy for poverty) and overcrowded housing [20-25]. These findings are consistent with models in which environmental stressors interact with developmentally sensitive biological systems.
Strengths
This analysis uses a large, population-based national dataset and introduces a developmental time-scale framework that aligns infants by biological age. By treating age as an alignment variable rather than a simple chronological measure, the approach aligns infants by developmental age while allowing differences in maturity at birth to be considered separately, and enables more precise localisation of risk transitions. The generalised additive model provides an intuitive visualisation of the developmental trajectory, while confirmatory windowed models translate these patterns into effect estimates that clinicians can readily interpret.
Limitations
Several limitations should be considered. First, analyses were restricted to PCA weeks 36–90 to maintain stable sex-specific counts. This excludes a small number of deaths at the extremes of the PCA distribution, including some extremely and very preterm infants who died early in postnatal life, and our findings should not be interpreted as implying absence of risk outside the analytic range. Second, the models included maternal smoking and preterm birth but did not account for postnatal exposures that may change during infancy, including breastfeeding. Residual influence from breastfeeding and other unmeasured postnatal factors therefore cannot be excluded. However, adjustment for such factors was not the primary objective of this analysis, which was to characterise the timing of the sex difference across developmental age rather than to estimate a fully adjusted causal effect of sex on SUID. Finally, as with all analyses based on vital-record data, exposure and cause-of-death misclassification are possible. 
Conclusions
Sex differences in SUID incidence are developmentally timed rather than constant across infancy, and this timing becomes evident when infants are aligned on a biologically meaningful time scale. Using PCA as an epidemiologic alignment variable localises the emergence of male excess after the developmental peak and reframes interpretation of risk patterns as functions of developmental timing rather than chronological age alone. This framework provides a basis for more precise counselling and prevention strategies and helps focus mechanistic research on the peri-peak developmental transition. In particular, if early-life stress exposure contributes to the emergence of male excess in SIDS, as suggested here, then interventions targeting socioeconomic and environmental stressors may represent an important avenue for reducing risk in infants beyond the early peak period.
